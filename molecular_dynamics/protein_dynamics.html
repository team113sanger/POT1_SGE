POT1 molecular dynamics

Exploratory simulations of wild-type POT1 and six missense variants bound to telomeric DNA.

Saturation genome editing places every POT1 missense variant on a quantitative functional scale, and separates the depleted variants into strong and weak classes. That classification is empirical: it says how a variant behaves in the assay, not why.

These simulations asked whether the distinction has a visible structural correlate — whether variants in different functional classes perturb the dynamics of the POT1–ssDNA complex in different ways. Seven systems were run, one microsecond each: wild type and six clinically observed variants, chosen to span the functional classes and two structural situations. Three contact the telomeric DNA directly; two sit away from it at positions important for the fold.

These are exploratory simulations shared alongside the paper. They are not part of its analysis and no result in the paper depends on them. The section below on what can be read from this sets out the register in which they are informative — and the register in which they are not.

The simulations

Individual structures show the first normal mode (NM1), with magnitude of motion for NM1 depicted by the width of the tube, for each key missense variant. Each structure is coloured as a heatmap based on the change in RMSF (ΔRMSF) with respect to the wild-type RMSF. Insets highlight significant changes in hydrogen bonding interactions (dashed lines) with the telomeric DNA with respect to wild-type, and changes in the intermolecular interactions as calculated from energy decomposition analysis (EDA) with respect to the corresponding mutation of each variant (individual values under each residue; positive values indicate interactions for wild-type are favoured and negative values indicate the variant is favoured).

The wild-type system is the reference throughout: it sets the baseline RMSF from which ΔRMSF is measured, and the hydrogen bonding and EDA values are compared against it.

What can be read from this

The useful part of this dataset is the setup, not the comparisons. Read in the right register it is informative; read as measurement it is not.

Each system was simulated once

With one trajectory per system there is no estimate of within-system variation, so a difference between two systems cannot be separated from the variation either would show on being run again. Everything comparative here — ΔRMSF, normal mode amplitude, hydrogen bond occupancy, EDA energies — is an observation to be tested rather than a quantity that has been measured. This constraint governs all the others.

The inset values carry no dispersion

They are single-trajectory numbers, so no uncertainty can be attached to them. EDA-derived interaction energies are in any case more reliable as relative than absolute quantities, and are strongest alongside experimental validation.

Convergence was not characterised

No RMSD-against-time analysis was performed, so whether 1 μs is long enough for these systems to equilibrate is not established.

Starting geometries were modelled

Mutant side chains were built with the Dunbrack rotamer library and clashes relieved. Rotamer choice matters at these positions, especially the three that contact DNA, so some of what is seen may follow from the initial model rather than from the substitution itself.

The analysis sits at the level of global motion

Normal modes and whole-chain RMSF describe how the complex moves overall. For substitutions like these the more informative level is local — what happens to a specific contact when a residue changes. The clearest case is p.Y89C, where the π–π stacking between Y89 and the telomeric guanine is lost outright. That is a concrete, tractable question this dataset is positioned to address but does not yet answer.

What the figure does show

Taken as orientation rather than as result: where the six substitutions sit relative to the DNA, which contacts each one is near, and which regions of the complex move most in the first normal mode. That is a reasonable starting point for designing a properly powered study.

Further work

The obvious next step is replication. Most of the cost of this kind of study is in building, parameterising and equilibrating the systems, and that work is done.

  • Multiple independent replicas per system, from different random velocity seeds — commonly more than five — so between-system differences can be separated from within-system variation. This single change converts every observation above into a testable quantity.
  • Convergence assessed and shown, with RMSD-against-time and related diagnostics, rather than inferred from trajectory length.
  • Local interaction analysis at the substituted positions, the Y89–guanine stacking being the first case to take: occupancy and geometry of that interaction across replicas, against wild type.
  • Sensitivity to the starting model, by sampling rotamer choice at the substituted positions rather than committing to a single built geometry.
  • Energetics with uncertainty, reporting hydrogen bonding and EDA-derived interaction energies across replicas and framing them as relative quantities.

Whether the strong and weak depleted classes have a structural basis is, on this evidence, an open question. It is a good question, and this is a reasonable place to start on it.

Methods as performed

EngineAMBER20, pmemd.cuda
Force fieldff19SB
Starting modelPDB 1XJV; Coot and MolProbity used to alleviate bad clashes
Missing residuesModeller interface in UCSF Chimera
Variant constructionDunbrack rotamer library in Chimera
SolvationTIP3P water, cubic box extending 12 Å from complex surface
CounterionsPotassium, to neutralise system charge
EnsembleNPT, Langevin thermostat
Non-bonded cutoff9 Å, smooth PME
Production length1 μs per system, 1 replica
Trajectory analysiscpptraj (RMSD, RMSF, hydrogen bonds)
Normal mode analysiscpptraj and ProDy (VMD)
Energy decompositionAMBER-EDA
Binding enthalpiesMM-GBSA via MMPBSA.py (AmberTools)
Downstream analysisR (data.table, tidyverse, abind)
VisualisationVMD, UCSF Chimera, ChimeraX, gnuplot, matplotlib, ggplot2
